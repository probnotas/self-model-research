What the runs show

  • Nothing told it the motor had changed. The repair is driven only by the mismatch between what it predicted and what its body did.
  • It repairs from its own failures. The 25 transitions are what it generated while trying to balance and falling over.
  • That matters more than the count: repairing from random flailing needs 500 samples for the same result, 20× more.
  • The model was learned without any reward function — just 2,000 transitions of self-generated motion. The policy it is measured against needed 20,000 steps of reward-driven training, and is tied to the one task it was trained for.

What they do not show

  • This is balance-hold, not swing-up from hanging. Swing-up is beyond this planner — it succeeds in under a quarter of attempts at every horizon we tried.
  • The repair is not perfect: 8 of 10 runs held upright at 25 samples, against 10 of 10 for the healthy body. It reaches 10 of 10 at 200 samples.
  • Simulation, one body, one kind of damage. A weakened actuator is the damage this planner is most sensitive to; a 50% mass increase barely dents it.
  • It does not beat reinforcement learning at staying upright. A model-free policy trained on the healthy body absorbed the same damage with no adaptation at all, holding 8 of 10 where ours held 0 of 10. The repair here is real; a claim that this outperforms RL is not.
